## Second Helpings

### Shopping tips

Lean proteins: chicken breast, 93/7 ground turkey/chicken, shrimp, white fish (cod/tilapia/mahi), salmon (the good omega-3 fat, worth spending on), eggs + egg whites, canned tuna.

High-protein sauce/topping bases: **2% cottage cheese** (creamy sauces) and **nonfat Greek yogurt** (cold applications, sour-cream swap). These two are the sleeper weapons: creamy texture, high protein, low fat.

Bulk/fiber: beans, lentils, rice. Snacks: jerky, Greek yogurt.

**Cottage cheese vs Greek yogurt, when to use which:** cottage cheese for _hot_ creamy sauces (blends silky, holds up to gentle heat, mild flavor). Greek yogurt for _cold_ uses (sour-cream swap, dip bases, tuna salad) since it curdles when heated and its tang fights savory hot sauces.

**Cooking oils:** avocado oil for high heat (blackening, searing). It has a high smoke point, is mostly monounsaturated fat, and is less refined than vegetable oil, which runs high in omega-6 (already overrepresented in most Western diets). The tradeoff is cost: avocado oil is pricier, so vegetable oil is a fine substitute if you're watching the budget. Olive oil for low-heat jobs and finishing (brushing bread, dressings) where its flavor is a plus, but not for true high-heat blackening, since it smokes and turns bitter.

### Nutritional Foundation

**The targets:** protein is set in grams first, about 0.7-1 g per lb of goal bodyweight (1.6-2.2 g/kg), a baseline that scales with the person, not a fixed number everyone converges on. Fat sits at 25-30% of calories and never drops below about 0.3 g per lb, enough to keep hormones and satiety in check without eating into the protein or carb budget. Carbs fill whatever's left. Protein and the fat floor track body weight, not calories, so their share of calories falls as calories rise and carbs take up the difference. That's why the goals below land on different ratios.

- **Balanced: 40 / 30 / 30 C/F/P.** Steady weight and general fitness. The neutral baseline the other goals are variations on.
- **Fat loss: 40 / 25 / 35 C/F/P.** A calorie deficit. Protein is a bigger share only because calories are lower. Keep meals moderate, up to about 550 calories.
- **Recomp: 45 / 25 / 30 C/F/P.** Calories at or slightly below maintenance. Carbs stay high enough to fuel training and protein stays high enough to protect muscle.
- **Clean bulk: 50 / 25 / 25 C/F/P.** A small surplus, about 250-300 calories, with the extra going mostly to carbs. Fat stays moderate to keep the gain lean.
- **Full bulk: 45 / 30 / 25 C/F/P.** A bigger surplus, about 500 calories. Protein grams stay put and the extra calories go mostly to fat, the easiest way to eat a lot. Look for dishes of 600 calories or more.
- **Carb cycling:** a higher-carb day (45% or more carbs) on heavy training days, a lower-carb day (35% or less) on rest days.

**Dishes versus the whole day:** these ratios describe a full day of eating. A single dish in this book rarely reaches the carb share of a bulk day, and it doesn't need to. Snacks and drinks, like fruit or a sports drink, are mostly carbs, so they pull the day toward the bulk ratios. A dish near 40 / 30 / 30 with a snack or a pre-workout drink can add up to a 50 / 25 / 25 day. The recipe filters rank dishes by how close they are to each goal and mark the good fits, instead of hiding the rest.

**Serving size:** servings are sized for eating 4-5 times throughout the day. That's often 3 meals plus a shake, drink, or snack (nuts, jerky, a protein bar, fruit) at other times. Protein is used best in moderate doses spread through the day, since one sitting has diminishing returns and a very large meal is hard to eat. A larger dinner with a fast-digesting shake keeps amino acids coming into the night.

Same skeleton, different dial. Protein is the one number that stays put in grams, and the percentages move around it as calories change.

**Why this book exists:** most comfort food blows the fat budget without buying you much protein for it. A real Alfredo can run 50g+ fat for barely more protein than plain pasta. The other direction has the opposite problem: a lot of "diet" or "healthy" recipes hit the macros by stripping the dish down until it stops tasting like the thing it's named after. Neither one is actually livable long-term: one wrecks the numbers, the other wrecks the food.

The bet here is that you don't have to choose. Every recipe in this book starts from a dish worth eating and gets rebuilt ingredient by ingredient: swap the fat-heavy component for a leaner base, then spend real effort putting the flavor back with aromatics, spice, and technique, instead of just serving a smaller, sadder portion of the original. The "traditional" row in every nutrition table is the receipt: same dish, real numbers, before and after.
